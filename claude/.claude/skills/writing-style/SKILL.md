## Voice principles (all registers)

**Open with the substance.** No throat-clearing, no scene-setting flourish: "Due diligence is pervasive." "A central issue in the bargaining literature is whether trade will be inefficiently delayed." "My research is at the intersection of game theory, information economics, and finance." First sentence states the thing; elaboration follows.

**One idea per paragraph, topic sentence first.** Mostly mid-length sentences with the subject and verb early; an occasional short sentence lands the point ("But is it economically important?" "My research shows that more is *not* necessarily better.").

**Motivate with concrete, named examples — not abstraction.** A startup that "caters" its innovation to a large acquirer; Wirecard's undetected accounting fraud; HP writing down $8.8bn on Autonomy; a used-car buyer downloading a vehicle history report. Give the specifics that make the example bite, and cite the source (often in a footnote).

**Questions do motivating work in prose.** A sequence of direct questions is a signature way to frame what a piece of work is about: "But is it economically important? What are the welfare implications? More specifically, how does the acquirer's ability to conduct due diligence affect deal terms, the likelihood of deal completion, the total surplus and how it is divided?" (Questions in running prose — never as section titles.)

**First person carries the claims.** "We propose," "we show," "we therefore model" in co-authored work; "I am interested in," "I seek to understand" in personal documents. Direct, no passive hedging around who is claiming what.

**Emphasis is surgical.** Italicize the single pivotal word (*not*, *only*, *regardless*, *hoping*, *strictly decrease*). Put a coined or informal term in quotes at first use ("news," "too much" due diligence, "experimentation," liquidity "dries up"), then let it work unquoted. Never bold for emphasis in prose.

**Hedge the interpretation, never the result.** Calibrated hedges appear deliberately and sparsely: "perhaps surprisingly," "arguably," "somewhat peculiar," "remarkably limited," "perhaps less obvious than one might think," "can likely be attributed to." Results are stated flat; it is the reading of them that gets a "perhaps."

**Contrast drives the argument.** State the expectation or common view, then turn: "There is a widespread belief that more information leads to better outcomes. My research shows that more is *not* necessarily better." "…which contrasts with the usual intuition that more competition leads to a more efficient outcome." Connectives of contrast — "However," "Yet," "In contrast," "Nevertheless," "Whereas," "Rather" — appear far more often than connectives of amplification.

**Enumerate in prose.** "We demonstrate three new results. First, … Second, … Finally, …" — not bullet lists. (Exception: slides; see Registers.)

**Connectives, sparing and natural:** "Thus," "Hence," "Therefore," "Indeed," "Of course," "That is," "Namely," "In particular," "Broadly speaking," "More specifically," "To fix ideas."

**Evidence over adjectives.** Claims are backed by numbers, citations, and named cases rather than intensifiers — even self-promotion is factual enumeration (citation counts, named syllabi, named prizes), never "outstanding" or "impactful."

**Err on the side of brevity.** If a sentence can be said in fewer words with the same meaning, the fewer-word version is better. Strip unnecessary adjectives and verbiage. Say each thing once: never restate a sentence in different words, and never make the same point twice in one document.

**Avoid jargon.** Field-standard terms are fine ("adverse selection," "identification"); buzzwords and needless technicality are not. Prefer the plain word.

**Footnotes do real work.** Caveats, robustness qualifications, practitioner sources, and asides go in footnotes (in documents that support them), keeping the main text clean.

**No contractions in formal documents** (papers, statements, letters to editors). Informal email can relax this.

## Tics to avoid (these read as generic/AI; the user does not write this way)

- **Punchy slogan headers** — bold run-ins or section titles that are mini-sentences ("Why one block is enough." "The two signs are one number."). Use plain topical headings or a topic sentence. Genuine structural run-ins ("Environment." "Preferences.") are fine.
- **Rhetorical-question section titles.** Questions belong in prose (see above), not in headings.
- **Metaphors and slogans:** "earns its keep," "the tell," "the trap," "the crux," "does the heavy lifting." Cut them; state the point.
- **Stacked em-dash asides.** An occasional em-dash for a definitional insertion is in-voice ("An undetected accounting fraud—a non-existing cash account—had left…"); piles of dramatic parentheticals are not.
- **Tricolons for rhythm** ("smoother, cheaper, and more resilient") and the **"not X — it's Y" reveal.**
- **Filler intensifiers:** "genuinely," "cleanly," "precisely," "exactly," "crucially," "importantly," "notably," "it is worth noting."
- **Bullet lists of prose points** (outside slides). Convert to flowing prose or a "First / Second / Finally" sentence; push caveats into footnotes.
- **Over-signposting** — restating each paragraph's conclusion at its start and end, or announcing "In this section we will…" for short sections.
- **Verbosity and echo.** Saying a sentence one way and then again another way; padding with words that add no meaning ("in order to," "the fact that," "it should be noted that"); adjectives that don't earn their place. Default AI prose is too verbose — cut it.
- **Jargon and buzzwords** where a plain word exists.
- **Generic platitudes:** "interesting paper," "makes a contribution," "this is an important topic," "the authors should clarify." Say the specific thing instead.

## Registers

- **Model/theory exposition** → use `model-writing` (it layers model-specific conventions on top of this skill).
- **Paper introductions:** phenomenon-first opening → concrete motivating example(s) → the question sequence → "In this paper, we propose…" → results preview built on contrasts, with the pivotal words italicized → brief roadmap ("In Section II, we explore…"). Match the two manuscript samples.
- **Editorial and referee correspondence** → the genre skills (`ae-recommendation`, `referee-report`, `strategic-revision`) govern structure and confidentiality; this skill governs the sentences.
- **First-person professional** (research/teaching statements, bios, award or grant materials): first-person singular, factual, evidence-backed; match the research statement.
- **Slides** → `conference-discussion` governs; bullets are the norm there, but sentence-level rules (no slogans, no filler intensifiers, surgical emphasis) still apply.
- **Email and informal notes:** no samples in the corpus yet — keep the same measured, plain, concrete voice one notch less formal; contractions acceptable. If the user adds email samples to `/Users/b.green/Git/sample_writing/`, calibrate against them.

## Workflow

1. Identify the register; if a genre skill covers it, follow that skill and use this one for the prose.
2. Read one corpus sample in the matching register to reset the ear.
3. Draft. Open with the substance; build paragraphs around one idea each; enumerate in prose.
4. Reread against "Tics to avoid" and strip anything that trips it. Then a brevity pass: cut every word whose removal leaves the meaning intact, and delete any sentence that repeats an earlier one.
5. Produce the prose; do not add editorial meta-commentary about the writing.
